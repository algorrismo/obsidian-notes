Since I know you're a **3rd-year Software Development student** and you already use **Obsidian**, I would **not** recommend relying mainly on paper. Paper is great for some subjects, but for university CS/SWE courses, Obsidian gives you much more long-term value.

Here's what I'd recommend.

## 🥇 Best Method: Obsidian + Active Recall + Spaced Revision (9.8/10)

This is the method many top university students and software engineers use.

### Step 1 — Create Complete Notes (Once)

After every lecture or from the class PDF:

```
Course
│
├── Week 01
├── Week 02
├── Week 03
│
└── Exam Notes
      ├── Cheat Sheet
      ├── Important Definitions
      ├── Frequently Asked Questions
      └── Common Mistakes
```

Each note should contain:

- Definitions
    
- Examples
    
- Diagrams
    
- Important points
    
- Tables
    
- Interview notes
    
- Exam tips
    

Only create these once.

---

## Step 2 — Highlight Important Parts

Don't highlight everything.

Use callouts like:

```md
> [!IMPORTANT]
> Definition of Functional Requirement

> [!TIP]
> This is frequently asked in quizzes.

> [!WARNING]
> Students often confuse Functional and Non-functional Requirements.
```

Now your eyes immediately find important content.

---

## Step 3 — Make Flash Questions

Instead of only reading:

```md
## Question

What is Encapsulation?

---

## Answer

Encapsulation means...
```

Or

```md
Q:
What are the four pillars of OOP?

A:
•
•
•
•
```

Your brain remembers questions much better than paragraphs.

---

## Step 4 — Don't Read Passively

Instead:

Close the note.

Ask yourself:

> "Can I explain this?"

If yes

Move on.

If not

Read again.

This is called **Active Recall** and it's one of the most effective study techniques.

✅ Math

✅ Algorithms

✅ Flowcharts

✅ Database normalization

✅ ER diagrams

✅ UML diagrams

✅ Dry-running code

That's slow and gives little benefit.

# Use the Feynman Technique

Read one topic.

Then pretend you're teaching a friend.

Example:

Instead of reading:

> Functional Requirement is...

Say out loud:

> "A functional requirement tells us what the system must do. For example, a login system must allow users to sign in."

If you can't explain it simply, you don't fully understand it yet.

---

# Use Spaced Repetition

Don't study a topic only once.

A good schedule is:

```
Day 1 → Learn

Day 2 → Review

Day 5 → Review

Day 10 → Review

Day 20 → Review

Before Quiz → Review

Before Mid → Review

Before Final → Review
```

Each review gets shorter because you're reinforcing what you've already learned.

# My recommended workflow for you

Since you already use **Obsidian** and often ask for detailed explanations, I'd suggest:

1. **Import the lecture PDF into Obsidian.**
    
2. **Use ChatGPT to convert it into comprehensive Markdown notes.**
    
3. **Read the notes once while highlighting key ideas.**
    
4. **Create a short "Revision" section at the end of each note** with:
    
    - 10–20 key definitions
        
    - 10 important questions
        
    - 5 common mistakes
        
    - 5 likely exam questions
        
5. **Review using Active Recall** instead of rereading.
    
6. **Use paper only for solving problems, drawing diagrams, writing algorithms, and practicing code.**
    

This approach gives you a permanent, searchable knowledge base in Obsidian that you can keep building throughout your degree, while also using study methods that are much more effective than repeatedly copying notes by hand.
