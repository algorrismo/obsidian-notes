Understanding how people express emotions through text is one of the most actively researched problems in modern natural language processing. As conversational AI systems become embedded in everyday life — from customer support bots to mental health apps — the need for accurate, context-aware sentiment and emotion modeling has grown substantially. [Multimodal Affective Computing Survey (2024)] The challenge is not simply detecting whether a message is positive or negative; real conversations involve layered emotional states, shifting sentiment across turns, and highly context-dependent language that basic classifiers completely fail [EmoLLMs — Liu et al. (2024)] to handle** .

The field has seen a dramatic shift since the release of large generative models. The GPT-4 technical report ** marked a turning point [GPT-4 Report — OpenAI (2023 ] it demonstrated that a single model trained on massive corpora could generalize across dozens of NLP tasks, including emotionally charged text, without any task-specific training. Shortly after, LLaMA 2 ** introduced open-source alternatives [LLaMA 2 — Touvron et al. (2023)] that gave researchers the ability to fine-tune and study model internals more freely. These developments pushed the research community to question whether purpose-built emotion classifiers were still necessary, or whether general-purpose LLMs had already surpassed them [Feng et al., ACL (2024)]**.

A comprehensive investigation by Zhao et al. **** directly studied this question by evaluating LLMs across 26 sentiment datasets, [Sentiment in LLM Era — NAACL (2024)] covering everything from basic polarity classification to aspect-based sentiment analysis. Their findings showed a clear pattern — LLMs perform well on simpler tasks but still fall short on complex sentiment phenomena that require structured or domain-specific understanding. This gap becomes even more visible in conversational settings, where a single utterance carries almost no meaning without the surrounding dialogue** . Emotion recognition in conversations (ERC) demands that models track speaker dynamics,
[ERC Survey (2025)] remember prior turns, and detect how emotional states shift naturally throughout a dialogue — a challenge that simple classification models were never designed for **.[MER Survey 2021–2025 (2026)]

To address this, researchers began building LLMs specifically tuned for emotional understanding. DialogueLLM **, proposed in 2023, fine-tuned LLaMA [DialogueLLM — Cheng et al. (2023)] using over 24,000 emotionally annotated conversational utterances drawn from MELD, IEMOCAP, and EmoryNLP. It introduced context-aware instruction tuning that explicitly models how emotions evolve within a conversation, and showed significant improvements over standard LLMs on all three benchmarks. Around the same time, the NUS-Emo system at SemEval-2024** demonstrated [NUS-Emo — Wang et al. (2024)] that emotion-cause-aware instruction tuning could allow LLMs to not only detect emotions but also reason about what caused them — a more sophisticated capability than standard classification.

Emotion-LLaMA **, presented at NeurIPS 2024,[Emotion-LLaMA — Cheng et al. (2024)] pushed this even further by aligning multimodal features from text, audio, and video into a shared representation space using a modified LLaMA backbone with instruction tuning. It achieved state-of-the-art results on the MER2023 challenge and zero-shot DFEW evaluation, suggesting that emotionally grounded LLMs are fast approaching the performance ceiling on established benchmarks** . More recently, a 2025 study on LLM-supervised pretraining **** showed that even without large labeled datasets, pseudo-labels generated by a text LLM from raw speech transcripts can train competitive emotion recognition models — opening a new direction for low-resource ERC research.[Li et al. (2025)]

Despite these advances, direct comparisons across LLMs remain inconsistent. A 2025 study benchmarking ChatGPT and DeepSeek  [Jovic et al. (2025)] highlighted that different models respond very differently to the same prompts, and that deep learning-based classifiers built on top of LLM embeddings still outperform raw generative outputs in several sentiment tasks. The survey on multimodal affective computing by Liang et al. ** further confirmed that the field lacks a unified evaluation framework, [Multimodal survey (2026)] with each paper using different datasets, metrics, and prompt formats, making cross-paper comparisons unreliable. A 2026 survey on multimodal emotion recognition **** reinforced this concern, noting that while transformer-based models like those built on MELD and IEMOCAP are reaching saturation, generalization to unseen domains and languages remains a largely unsolved problem [State-of-art MER (2026)].

Recent work has also demonstrated that model architecture alone is not the determining factor — training strategy matters just as much. Emotion-Aware RoBERTa **, published in 2025, achieved 96.77% [Hassan et al. (2025)] accuracy by introducing an emotion-specific attention layer and a TF-IDF gating mechanism on top of an existing pretrained model, outperforming much larger LLMs on the same datasets. This suggests that targeted architectural modifications can close the gap with massive models at a fraction of the computational cost** . Similarly, a 2026 hybrid model combining LSTM and RoBERTa **** demonstrated that combining sequential modeling with deep contextual representations consistently improves emotion detection, especially for rare and nuanced emotional categories. [Nature hybrid model (2026)]

On the multimodal front, a recent IEEE study on transforming multimodal ERC ** showed that cross-modal attention between text and audio leads to significant F1 gains on MELD and IEMOCAP, and that LLMs serving as backbone encoders outperform CNN and BiLSTM baselines by substantial margins.[IEEE multimodal ERC (2025)] The 2025 survey on multimodal emotion recognition in conversations  further outlined that while unimodal text-based models are still widely used, fusion-based approaches that incorporate acoustic and visual signals are rapidly becoming the new standard for production-level systems.[EMNLP survey (2025)] A 2026 multimodal baseline study for SemEval-2024 Task 3  confirmed that even lightweight fusion of text and speech embeddings using transformer classifiers consistently outperforms unimodal approaches on conversation-level emotion understanding.[SemEval-2024 baseline (2026)]
