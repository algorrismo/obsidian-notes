## Before we start: a look at your own English

You already gave me something more useful than a list of weaknesses — a real writing sample. Let's use it, not to find fault, but because seeing *your* patterns is far more useful than generic grammar rules. Here are four moments from your message, and the pattern behind each one.

You wrote

“i strutted when i wants to speak”

Cleaner

“I stutter when I want to speak”

The pattern: with I / you / we / they, the verb never takes an -s ending — only he / she / it does (he wants, but I want). And in English, the word “I” is always a capital letter, with no exceptions, ever.

You wrote

“whats dose this word has a meaning”

Cleaner

“what this word means”

The pattern: once a question is tucked inside a bigger sentence, English quietly flips back to normal subject-then-verb order. This is one of the trickiest rules in the whole language — it catches out even strong learners, so don't read too much into missing it.

You wrote

“i feels much tensed”

Cleaner

“I feel very tense”

The pattern: “tense” is the describing word you want; “much” pairs with things you can't count one by one (much water), while “very” pairs with feelings.

You wrote

“i genuinely make soo much mistakes”

Cleaner

“I genuinely make so many mistakes”

The pattern: you can count mistakes one by one (one mistake, two mistakes), so it takes “many,” not “much.” The same rule as the example above, from the other direction.

I also noticed words like *srtucture*, *peparaing*, and *wrting* — these read less like gaps in your knowledge and more like typing-speed slips, the kind that happen when your hand moves faster than your proofreading. That has an easy, mechanical fix, which is in the Writing section below.

**Here's something worth sitting with:** two of the patterns above exist specifically because your first language is Bangla, not because of any weakness in you. First, Bangla script has no capital or lowercase letters at all — so English capitalization isn't an instinct you're missing, it's a brand-new rule you have to consciously build into a habit. Second, Bangla sentences are typically built Subject–Object–Verb (“আমি ভাত খাই” — literally “I rice eat”), while English is Subject–Verb–Object (“I eat rice”). Every English sentence you produce means overriding your first language's default word order, in real time. That is genuinely demanding mental work — it's not a sign that you don't know English.

One more honest observation: read back over your own message. You expressed cause and effect, self-doubt, multiple examples per problem, using compound and layered sentences — not just simple ones. That is not beginner-level thinking. You are not “new to English.” You're an intermediate learner (roughly B1 on the international CEFR scale) with a real foundation, working on specific, nameable habits. That distinction matters, because the plan for “fixing habits” looks completely different from the plan for “building a foundation from zero” — and you're further along than you're giving yourself credit for.

## How the exam is actually built

It helps to train for the exam you'll actually sit, not a vague idea of it. Since you're preparing for higher studies abroad, I'm assuming **IELTS Academic** rather than General Training — Listening and Speaking are identical either way, but Academic Reading and Writing use more research- and journal-style material.

| Section | Time | What happens | Scored on |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Listening | 30 min, plus time to transfer answers | 4 recorded sections, each played once only, 40 questions, gets harder as it goes — the final section is an academic-style lecture | Number of correct answers |
| Reading | 60 min | 3 passages, 40 questions, academic and journal-style texts | Number of correct answers |
| Writing | 60 min | Task 1: describe a chart, graph, or diagram objectively, 150 words minimum. Task 2: an essay giving your opinion, 250 words minimum, worth roughly twice as much as Task 1 | Task achievement, coherence & cohesion, vocabulary, grammar |
| Speaking | 11–14 min, face to face or by video | Part 1: interview on familiar topics. Part 2: one minute to prepare, then speak alone for up to two minutes on a card. Part 3: a deeper discussion on the same theme | Fluency & coherence, vocabulary, grammar, pronunciation |

Listening, Reading, and Writing always run back to back on the same day with no break in between — over two and a half hours of sustained focus. Speaking may happen the same day, or up to a week before or after, depending on your test centre.

**On scoring:** each of the four skills gets its own band from 0 to 9. Your overall band is simply the average of the four, rounded in your favour — an average ending in .25 rounds up to the next half band (6.25 becomes 6.5), and .75 rounds up to the next whole band (6.75 becomes 7.0). There's no weighting between skills, which means a weak section pulls your average down exactly as much regardless of which one it is. Balanced effort across all four beats being excellent in one and neglecting another.

**Worth knowing:** the British Council's *IELTS One Skill Retake* is available in Bangladesh for the computer-delivered test. If one section badly under-represents you on the day, you can retake just that section within 60 days instead of sitting the whole exam again. It's accepted by a large and growing number of institutions, but not all of them — if you're counting on it as a safety net, confirm your target university accepts it before you rely on it.

## Listening

40 questions across 4 sections, played once only, getting harder as it goes. Section 4 is an academic lecture — exactly like the faculty lectures you mentioned struggling with.

### Why this feels hard

Listening in a second language demands far more mental effort than listening in your first. Your brain has to decode sound into words, words into meaning, and hold that meaning in mind while the next sentence is already arriving — all in real time. In your first language, most of that is automatic. In English, right now, very little of it is. That's genuinely tiring, and tired attention wanders. This isn't a discipline problem, it's a processing-load problem, and the fix isn't “concentrate harder.” It's training your ear the way you'd train a muscle: gradually, with the right kind of repeated practice.

### Your roadmap

- [ ] **Build listening stamina gradually.** Start with 3–5 minute clips daily and work up toward 10–15 minute lecture-length audio over several weeks. Don't jump straight to long lectures — that's like sprinting before you can jog.
- [ ] **Read the questions before the audio plays.** The real test gives you preview time — use every second of it. Predict what kind of answer is needed (a name? a number? a place?) before you hear anything, so your brain knows what to listen *for* instead of processing everything equally.
- [ ] **Take skeleton notes, not full sentences.** Practise writing only keywords while listening — dates, names, numbers, the last word of an idea. Full sentences are too slow to write in real time.
- [ ] **Learn signpost language.** Phrases like “the most important factor is,” “in contrast,” or “to summarize” tell you something worth noting is coming next. Noticing these often matters more than understanding every single word.
- [ ] **Practise shadowing daily.** Play a short clip of clear English (30–60 seconds), pause, and repeat exactly what you heard, copying the rhythm and stress, not just the words. Ten minutes a day trains your ear and your mouth together.
- [ ] **Let go of missed answers immediately.** Dwelling on one miss is what causes the next two or three to be missed too, because your attention is stuck in the past while new audio keeps arriving. Practise consciously thinking “next one” and moving on.
- [ ] **Practise under real, one-shot conditions.** Use official practice tests, play the audio once only, no pausing or rewinding — so the pressure of “no second chances” becomes familiar instead of panic-inducing on the actual day.

Start this week

Pick one short podcast or video — five minutes, clear English, a topic you actually like. Listen once for the general idea. Listen again taking keyword notes only. Repeat daily for a week before adding anything else.

## Reading

60 minutes, 3 passages, 40 questions, and deliberately more text than most people can fully read in the time given.

### Why this feels hard

You said you get lost not knowing what a word means in context — and that's actually the exact skill IELTS Reading is testing, more than raw vocabulary size. The passages are written above most test-takers' comfort level on purpose. Trying to understand every single word is the trap, not the solution — even highly fluent readers don't know every word in an academic text. The real skill is pulling out enough meaning to answer the question, using the words around an unfamiliar one as evidence.

### Your roadmap

- [ ] **Guess meaning from context on purpose.** When you hit an unknown word, check: is it a noun, verb, or adjective (that alone narrows the meaning)? Are there clue words nearby like “however,” “for example,” or “which means”? Does the word contain a recognisable part, like *un-* (not) or *-tion* (a noun)?
- [ ] **Drill that instinct directly.** Take any short English article, underline five unfamiliar words, guess their meaning from context only, then check a dictionary. Three times a week, this trains exactly what IELTS rewards.
- [ ] **Read daily without stopping for every word.** Spend 20–30 minutes on English content you actually enjoy. First pass: read for the general idea, no dictionary. Second pass: look up only the 5–10 words that seem to matter most.
- [ ] **Learn the question-type traps.** True/False/Not Given is about stated facts; Yes/No/Not Given is about the writer's opinions. “Not Given” means the passage simply doesn't say — it is not the same as “False.” Mixing these up is one of the most common ways to lose easy marks.
- [ ] **Skim before you scan.** Spend the first minute on a passage reading only the first and last sentence of each paragraph, to build a mental map before hunting for specific answers. This saves far more time than reading start to finish.
- [ ] **Practise with a timer, every time.** Twenty minutes per passage, from your very first practice session — your internal clock matters as much as your comprehension.
- [ ] **Keep a living vocabulary log.** For every new word: the sentence you found it in, a simple synonym, and your own example sentence. Review it with spaced repetition (an app like Anki works well) rather than a list you write once and never revisit.

Start this week

Take one article on a topic you enjoy. Read it once for the gist, no dictionary. Then go back and guess — don't look up — the meaning of five words you didn't know, using only the sentence around them. Check yourself afterward.

## Writing

60 minutes, two tasks. Task 1 (150 words minimum) objectively describes a chart, graph, or diagram — no opinion allowed. Task 2 (250 words minimum) is a full essay giving your opinion, worth roughly twice as much of your Writing band.

### Why this feels hard

You described it precisely: you have the ideas, but you don't know how to turn them into sentences. This is one of the most common — and most fixable — struggles for Bangla speakers specifically, for a structural reason. Bangla builds sentences Subject–Object–Verb (“ami bhat khai” — “I rice eat”), while English builds them Subject–Verb–Object (“I eat rice”). Every English sentence you write means overriding your first language's automatic word order. On top of that, Bangla doesn't use articles the way English uses “a,” “an,” and “the,” which is exactly why they're the easiest thing to drop or misplace. None of this reflects how many ideas you have — it reflects that the packaging system is still under construction, and packaging is teachable in a very concrete, mechanical way.

### Your roadmap

- [ ] **Drill the basic sentence engine until it's automatic.** Subject + Verb + Object is English's default pattern. Practise consciously rebuilding a Bangla thought into that order in English, out loud or on paper, before attempting anything more complex.
- [ ] **Practise sentence-combining, not just sentence-writing.** Take two simple sentences and join them three ways: with and/but/so, with because/although/when, and by turning one into a describing phrase. Repeated often, this is one of the fastest ways to raise a grammar score.
- [ ] **Never start writing without a 3–5 minute skeleton.** Jot your position and two main points before Task 2; jot the two or three biggest trends before Task 1. Writing without this is exactly what causes “I have ideas but can't get them out” — your ideas are competing with sentence-building in real time.
- [ ] **Use a fixed shape for each task.** Task 2: introduction (restate the question + your position) → body 1 (idea, explanation, example) → body 2 (second idea, explanation, example) → conclusion. Task 1: introduction (restate the chart's title) → overview (2 sentences on the biggest patterns — the single most under-used sentence in Task 1) → body paragraphs with specific data.
- [ ] **Separate your editing passes.** After writing, re-read once only for grammar and spelling, then once only for whether you fully answered the question. Trying to catch everything in one pass is why small errors slip through — you can't proofread and compose at the same time.
- [ ] **Watch for the specific patterns common to Bangla-to-English writing:** missing or extra articles (a/an/the), subject-verb agreement (he go → he goes), and mixed verb tenses within one paragraph. These three alone account for a large share of avoidable errors.
- [ ] **Build vocabulary by topic, in word families.** Task 2 recycles a small set of themes — education, environment, technology, health, work, government. For each, learn 10–15 words as families (govern → government → governmental) plus natural pairings like “make a decision,” not “do a decision.”
- [ ] **Get real feedback, not just practice volume.** Writing without feedback lets errors settle in. Paste a practice essay to me any time — I'll mark it against the real IELTS criteria and show you exactly what to fix, the same way I just did with your message above.

Start this week

Write one Task 2 introduction only — just restating a practice question in your own words plus your opinion, two to three sentences. Do this daily for a week before attempting a full essay. Getting the opening right removes a lot of the blank-page pressure.

## Speaking

11–14 minutes: Part 1 is a short interview on familiar topics; Part 2 gives you a card, one minute to prepare, then up to two minutes speaking alone; Part 3 is a deeper two-way discussion on the same theme.

### Why this feels hard

What you're calling “stuttering” is, for almost every second-language speaker, not a speech problem — it's your brain trying to plan grammar and vocabulary in real time while also speaking out loud, which is a genuinely heavy mental load. It eases not by “trying to relax,” but by making more of your English automatic, so there's less left to consciously plan mid-sentence. And to be direct about one thing in your message: not being sure how a word is spelled has nothing to do with speaking — spelling is never tested in the Speaking section. But not feeling sure of a word's exact form does often make people hesitant to say it out loud. The fix for both is the same: learn new words as a full family — how it's spelled, how it sounds, how it's used — so a word feels fully owned, not half-known.

### Your roadmap

- [ ] **Talk to yourself, out loud, daily.** Narrate ordinary activities in English while you do them (“Now I'm making tea. First I boil the water…”) — no audience, no pressure, pure automaticity practice. Five to ten minutes a day makes a real difference over a few weeks.
- [ ] **Record and replay yourself weekly.** Answer a real Part 2 cue card out loud, recorded, for two minutes. Listen back once for content, once only for hesitations. This desensitises you to your own voice and shows you exactly where you stall.
- [ ] **Learn thinking-time phrases on purpose.** Pausing to think is normal — examiners don't penalise a thoughtful pause, only long silence or constant “um.” Phrases like “That's an interesting question, let me think,” buy real time while sounding natural.
- [ ] **Build vocabulary as complete families, not single words.** For any new word, learn to spell it, say it aloud, and use it in one sentence of your own, all at once. Half-known words are exactly what makes people freeze mid-sentence.
- [ ] **Use a simple shape for Part 2.** Point (what it is) → reason or detail (why or how) → example (a specific moment) → point again (briefly restate). This turns “what do I even say” panic into “which slot am I filling.”
- [ ] **Practise Part 3 with real reasoning chains.** Use structures like “There are a few reasons for this. Firstly… Secondly… For example…” — Part 3 specifically rewards extended, connected reasoning over short direct answers.
- [ ] **Find a regular speaking partner.** A study group, a tutor, or a language exchange app (Tandem, HelloTalk, and italki are common choices) — real back-and-forth conversation builds fluency in a way solo practice can't.
- [ ] **Practise under real timing, weekly.** One minute to prepare, two minutes to speak, timed, no exceptions — building comfort with the exact clock you'll face on test day.

Start this week

Pick one everyday activity — making tea, walking to class, cooking — and narrate it out loud in English, alone, for three minutes a day. It will feel strange for about three days. That's normal. Keep going.

## A sample weekly rhythm

All four skills feed each other, and your final score is an average of all four — so touching each one every week matters more than perfecting one while neglecting the rest. Adjust the days to fit your own schedule; the pattern is what matters.

| Day | Focus | Activity (30–60 min) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mon | Listening | Shadowing (10 min) + guided listening practice (20 min) |
| Tue | Reading | Timed passage (20 min) + context-guessing drill (10 min) |
| Wed | Writing | Task 2 planning + one full paragraph, with a self-edit pass |
| Thu | Speaking | Self-talk narration (10 min) + recorded Part 2 practice |
| Fri | Reading + vocabulary | Extensive reading (20–30 min) + vocabulary log review |
| Sat | Full timed practice | One full timed section, rotating through the 4 skills week to week |
| Sun | Review + rest | Light review of the week's vocabulary log; an easier day |
