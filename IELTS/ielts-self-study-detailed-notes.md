## 1. The central message and the two-month qualification

**Reference: [00:00–01:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0m1DI4YCrEc&t=0s).**

The video addresses people preparing for IELTS, considering starting, or wondering whether they can succeed through independent study at home.

Maruf reports an overall **Band 8**, following approximately **two months of IELTS-specific preparation**. He says he prepared independently at home without a coaching centre. He identifies himself as a madrasa student, rather than someone educated in an English-medium school or surrounded by English from childhood.

The qualification that matters most is that **he already had an English foundation before those two months**. He explicitly says his English was not built from scratch during that period. The two months were devoted to applying and developing an existing ability for IELTS.

His opening plan has three parts: describe his own journey, explain how to improve English and prepare independently, and identify online resources.

He presents coaching as unnecessary for his approach and emphasizes access to a phone and online material. Later, he also recommends obtaining Cambridge practice books. Consequently, the practical message is that coaching fees are avoidable; the video should not be read as a precise promise that every preparation expense is zero.

He says scores such as 7, 7.5, or 8 require work but are achievable. He repeatedly links progress to sustained effort and consistency. His statements guaranteeing high scores are motivational claims, not evidence that any fixed routine will produce the same result for every learner.

## 2. His English-learning journey

**Reference: [01:39–05:37](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0m1DI4YCrEc&t=99s).**

### 2.1 Writing regularly

He began keeping a diary in English. His initial aim was to write something regularly rather than wait for a perfect subject or advanced language. This made producing English an ordinary activity.

The auto-generated captions do not clearly preserve the school class in which he began; these notes do not guess it.

### 2.2 Reading about existing interests

He did not describe beginning with a large reading list of difficult books. Instead, he read English articles about subjects he already liked, particularly technology. He replaced Bengali material on those subjects with English material.

He associates this habit with better reading comprehension. Interest in the subject supplied a reason to continue reading even when English itself was not his main priority.

### 2.3 Changing everyday media consumption

He watched podcasts and shifted the content he consumed into English. Listening to English improved his ability to understand speech, while following subtitles also contributed to reading practice.

### 2.4 Thinking in English

He presents this as the most influential habit in his own development.

He deliberately tried to conduct his internal thoughts in English. At first, he could not do this smoothly and had to keep reminding himself to switch from Bengali. He used whatever English he had, including imperfect English. With repetition, he says English thoughts became more automatic.

His explanation is that an internal English conversation gives repeated practice in forming ideas and expressing them. When he eventually spoke with another person, it felt less like creating English for the first time: he had already rehearsed expressing thoughts internally many times.

This is his personal account of what helped him. It accompanies actual speaking practice elsewhere in the video.

### 2.5 Recording himself and reviewing the result

He made video and audio notes about subjects such as:

- What he had done that day.
- His plans for the following day.
- His life goals.
- His dreams and ambitions.

He then replayed the recordings and considered his mistakes, hesitation, overall speaking ability, and most noticeable weaknesses. Recording gave him material to examine rather than relying only on how fluent he felt while speaking.

### 2.6 Learning around other responsibilities

English was not his sole academic priority. He also studied Arabic and other madrasa subjects. He lived in a hostel and did not always have access to a phone, so recording and watching material were not always possible.

He describes this earlier English development as part-time. Thinking in English was especially useful to him because it was available even without a device.

He places the start of dedicated IELTS preparation around the middle of **2024**. At the beginning of the video, he says the exam was around September of the previous year; given the April 2025 publication date, that points to approximately **September 2024**. These are approximate descriptions, not an exact dated study calendar.

## 3. IELTS overview

**Reference: [05:38–06:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0m1DI4YCrEc&t=338s).**

He distinguishes IELTS Academic from IELTS General Training. His simplified explanation associates Academic with students going abroad and General Training with people going abroad for work. **This video focuses on Academic.**

The four skills are Reading, Listening, Speaking, and Writing.

| Skill | Format described in the video | Main preparation emphasis |
|---|---|---|
| Reading | Three passages, 40 questions, 60 minutes; no additional answer-transfer time | Comprehension, speed, and mistake analysis |
| Listening | Four parts, 40 questions, approximately 30 minutes of audio; he also describes 10 minutes for answer transfer | Understanding speech and following the recording without losing your place |
| Speaking | Three parts: short questions, an extended response, and related discussion | Natural conversation, confidence, recorded practice |
| Academic Writing | A response to visual information and an essay; he mentions 150 and 250 words | Timed practice, suitable organization, clear expression |

**Editorial note:** His Listening transfer-time explanation refers to paper-based testing. The word counts are minimums, not exact required lengths. See [Section 11](#11-clarifications-for-accurate-future-reference) before using this table as an exam-day reference.

## 4. Reading: foundation, technique, and test practice

**Reference: [06:04–13:21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0m1DI4YCrEc&t=364s).**

### 4.1 Why Reading can feel difficult

Candidates must understand three passages and answer 40 questions within 60 minutes. He identifies time management as a major difficulty because passages can contain unfamiliar vocabulary and complicated sentence structures.

He says that with practice he could finish in approximately **50–52 minutes**. This is his reported performance, not a target he proves everyone must meet.

He mentions roughly 11 or 12 Reading question types but does not teach them individually in this video. He says that would require a separate explanation.

### 4.2 Build comprehension before relying on practice tests

His readiness threshold is being able to read an English passage and understand at least its main point. You need not understand every word, but you should be able to follow what the text is broadly saying.

His reasoning is straightforward: if the passage itself is incomprehensible, answering questions about it will be difficult regardless of how many practice tests you attempt.

### 4.3 What to read

Read English articles, books, or other material regularly. Begin with topics you enjoy rather than forcing yourself through subjects you dislike.

His examples include sports articles and English newspapers for sports enthusiasts, and beauty or makeup material for someone interested in those subjects. His own example is technology.

He argues that enjoyable material makes practice feel more like pursuing an interest and less like compulsory schoolwork. This makes the learning process easier to sustain. He describes IELTS preparation as a continuing journey whose quality depends partly on how you approach it.

### 4.4 How to handle unfamiliar words

He advises against interrupting reading to look up every unknown word.

1. Read the whole sentence and surrounding context.
2. Try to infer the unfamiliar word’s meaning.
3. Continue if you still understand the main message.
4. Look up the word when it is essential to understanding the sentence or main idea.

The distinction is between an unknown word that merely adds detail and one that blocks comprehension. His objection is to constant interruption, not to all dictionary use.

### 4.5 Cambridge practice books

He recommends Cambridge IELTS books for exam practice. The upper number is unclear in the auto-captions, but the description explicitly lists **Cambridge 8–19**. He explains that each contains four practice tests with answers for checking work, describing them as real IELTS exam material.

This is the creator’s 2025 book range, not a claim about the newest available volume today.

### 4.6 His timed Reading practice method

1. Choose a Cambridge Reading test.
2. Start a timer and attempt all 40 questions.
3. At 60 minutes, record how many you completed.
4. Finish the remaining questions even if you exceed the limit during learning practice.
5. Record the total completion time.
6. Work toward reducing that time in later tests.

He acknowledges that an early attempt may take around two hours or longer. He suggests gradual reductions, such as five or ten minutes, rather than expecting immediate speed.

The captions are loose about whether his two-hour example refers to a passage or the larger attempt. The dependable point is to track completion time and improve it progressively.

### 4.7 Reviewing errors is essential

Checking the total score is insufficient. For each wrong answer, identify what went wrong and why. Try to prevent the same type of mistake in the next test.

He emphasizes that the error may recur several times. The response is to examine it again and continue correcting it until the pattern weakens. Consistency matters more than expecting a single explanation to eliminate a habit immediately.

At approximately **13:08–13:20**, he mentions a personal Reading technique that helped him and some people he advised. **He does not explain that technique in this video**; he reserves it for a dedicated video.

## 5. Listening: exposure and exam technique

**Reference: [13:22–18:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0m1DI4YCrEc&t=802s).**

### 5.1 Understand spoken English before focusing only on test answers

As with Reading, he recommends building the underlying skill first. If you cannot understand what is being said, you cannot reliably extract answers from it.

The foundation is regular listening to English that interests you. Examples include English comedy, sports commentary, and English-language cooking tutorials.

### 5.2 Make English part of ordinary media use

He suggests using time already spent on social media as additional English exposure. The activity can still be entertaining while providing listening practice.

To encourage English recommendations, he proposes either a new email/account or clearing previous YouTube history, then subscribing to and watching English channels in your preferred subjects. This records his suggestion; no account or history changes were performed while preparing this document.

His broader aim is that opening a phone or social platform should frequently lead to English content without requiring a separate decision to study every time.

### 5.3 Practising with Cambridge audio

Once you can follow spoken English reasonably well:

1. Choose a Listening test from a Cambridge book.
2. Locate its matching audio. His example is searching YouTube for **Cambridge 8 Listening 1**.
3. Prepare answers numbered 1–40 in a notebook, or write in the spaces in the book.
4. Play the audio and answer as you listen.
5. Complete the attempt without pausing or rewinding.

He calls the matching recording the official audio. When using a search result, make sure it actually corresponds to the book and test number you selected.

### 5.4 Do not let one missed answer become several

If you miss a question, move forward. Continuing to think about the missed answer can make you miss the next one while the recording continues.

Before each part begins, read that part’s questions and mark useful keywords. He does not mean that you must read every question in the entire test at once. The purpose is to know what information you are listening for and remain oriented as the audio progresses.

### 5.5 Review by error pattern and question type

After checking answers, ask which answers were wrong, why they were wrong, which section or question type caused the most difficulty, and what would improve the next attempt.

He loosely refers to around 12 or 13 Listening question types. He does not enumerate them or teach a strategy for each one. Treat that count as an informal description, not an official classification.

He also says additional Listening techniques exist but are outside this introductory video’s scope.

## 6. Speaking: thinking, conversation, and exam practice

**Reference: [18:16–23:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0m1DI4YCrEc&t=1096s).**

### 6.1 Two habits working together

He presents Speaking as relatively approachable to develop through frequent use. His two repeated habits are speaking as much as possible and thinking in English throughout ordinary life.

### 6.2 Practise normal conversation across many topics

He advises against making every conversation a sequence of IELTS interview questions. Ordinary conversations can move between friends, family, life, studies, humour, philosophy, and other interests.

His reasoning is that topic variety develops the ability to respond when the examiner asks something unexpected. You gain experience expressing ideas across subjects instead of depending on a narrow collection of rehearsed answers.

He does **not** say to abandon IELTS Speaking practice. He recommends general conversation and exam-style practice alongside each other.

### 6.3 Record exam-style responses

Use a two-minute timer and speak on one topic. Alternatively, ask another person to read questions while you record your responses. If no partner is available, ask and answer the questions yourself.

Practise all three Speaking parts. Replay the recording and identify mistakes, their causes, and ways to improve the next response.

### 6.4 Begin with enough grammar to communicate

For a complete beginner, he recommends basic grammar such as tenses and sentence structure. He does not think a learner must master a large grammar syllabus before beginning to speak.

During early conversation, concentrate on expressing your intended meaning. Use simple words when necessary instead of stopping repeatedly to search for impressive vocabulary or perfect grammar.

After you can communicate more comfortably, expand vocabulary and practise phrases, clauses, different sentence structures, and active/passive constructions.

This is a proposed learning sequence. It is not a claim that grammar or vocabulary do not matter in IELTS assessment.

### 6.5 Places and methods for practice

He names **Lingbe** and **Hilokal**, whose spellings are confirmed by the description. He describes joining groups or community conversations, speaking with different people, and encountering different topics. Near the end, he also mentions one-to-one conversation.

He suggests conversing with ChatGPT, making audio or video notes, and becoming more observant about your own performance through playback.

### 6.6 Speaking test structure as he explains it

| Part | Description in the video |
|---|---|
| Part 1 | Several short questions and answers |
| Part 2 | A topic for an extended response of about two minutes, preceded by one minute to prepare notes |
| Part 3 | Follow-up discussion connected to the Part 2 topic; he informally describes around three or four questions |

The Part 3 question count is his rough explanation, not a guaranteed fixed number.

### 6.7 Confidence and the preparation minute

He suggests mentally treating the examiner as a friendly person having a conversation with you. This is intended to reduce anxiety and make expression easier.

He also encourages face-to-face practice because speaking with someone physically present can develop confidence for the interview.

For Part 2, use the preparation minute actively. Write keywords that remind you of the ideas you want to cover. A useful keyword should recall a larger point so that you can keep speaking without writing a complete script first.

## 7. Writing: preparation, feedback, and organization

**Reference: [23:36–26:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0m1DI4YCrEc&t=1416s).**

### 7.1 His own preparation mistake

He considered his general writing good enough and initially took IELTS Writing for granted. He identifies this as a major mistake: ordinary writing ability does not automatically produce effective IELTS answers under time limits.

He treats Writing as challenging but improvable with focused preparation.

### 7.2 Write frequently and obtain feedback

He recommends diaries or writing about subjects you like to build the habit of expressing ideas in English.

For feedback, he suggests asking ChatGPT to identify mistakes or improve a piece of writing. He also names **QuillBot** and **Grammarly** as tools that can highlight errors and possible corrections.

The learning purpose is to understand what went wrong and how to improve your own writing, rather than simply receive a revised paragraph.

### 7.3 Grammar topics displayed on screen

The speaker refers to an on-screen list at approximately **24:48–24:54**. The recovered list is recorded in [Section 9](#9-resources-shown-or-mentioned).

### 7.4 Start IELTS-specific Writing practice early

Alongside general writing, practise IELTS responses from the beginning of exam preparation. Otherwise, time management and answer organization may remain weak even if you are comfortable writing informally.

He describes Academic Task 1 as explaining visual information such as a graph or diagram, and Task 2 as writing an essay on a given subject. He refers to 150 words and 250 words respectively. These are minimum requirements; Task 1 is better understood as a report or description rather than the same type of essay as Task 2.

### 7.5 Organization and idea selection

He emphasizes suitable structure because it makes a response clearer and easier for the examiner to follow. Different essay questions call for different ways of organizing the answer. He does not supply detailed essay structures in this video.

When generating ideas, choose relevant ideas you can explain clearly. He advises against selecting complicated ideas merely to display knowledge: IELTS is assessing language, and a simple idea expressed and developed well is more useful than an elaborate idea you cannot explain.

## 11. Clarifications for accurate future reference

**This section is editorial context, separate from the video’s teaching. Official-format references were checked on 27 September 2026.**

### Two months does not mean English from zero to Band 8

His earlier reading, writing, listening, thinking, and recording habits are part of the background to the result. Do not remove that background when interpreting the title. Likewise, his six-month encouragement does not establish a predictable score for every starting level.

### Academic and General Training differ in particular components

The video’s student/work distinction is a broad introduction. Official IELTS material confirms that Academic and General Training share Listening and Speaking, while Reading and Writing differ. Determine the required test from the institution or organisation receiving the result. [Official IELTS sample-test information](https://www.ielts.org/take-a-test/preparation-resources/sample-test-questions/academic-test).

### Listening transfer time depends on delivery format

The additional ten minutes he describes concerns transferring answers in paper-based Listening. Do not add that time to a computer practice test. Current IELTS information describes computer delivery, with an optional handwritten Writing component in selected countries; that option does not make Listening paper-based. Use the instructions for your booked test. [British Council guide describing paper-based transfer time](https://www.britishcouncil.rs/sites/default/files/ielts_guide_for_test_takers_web.pdf), [IELTS Writing on Paper option](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-on-computer-writing-on-paper).

### Writing word counts, timing, and assessment

Academic Writing requires **at least 150 words for Task 1 and at least 250 for Task 2** within 60 minutes overall. IELTS suggests approximately 20 minutes for Task 1 and 40 for Task 2. Task 2 carries twice the weight of Task 1. Clear ideas matter, but so do fulfilling the question, organizing the response, vocabulary, and grammatical range and accuracy. [Official IELTS Academic Writing format](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-academic-test/ielts-academic-format-writing).

### Speaking is assessed beyond confidence

Confidence and regular conversation can support practice, but official assessment includes fluency/coherence, vocabulary, grammatical range/accuracy, and pronunciation. Speaking lasts 11–14 minutes overall. Part 2 includes one minute to prepare; Part 3 is a 4–5 minute discussion, not a guaranteed set of three or four questions. [Official IELTS Speaking format](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-academic-test/ielts-academic-format-speaking).

### Informal question-type counts are not official totals

The speaker gives approximate Reading and Listening counts without defining his categories. For example, the official Listening page groups tasks under six headings, some of which contain several variants. Do not memorize his approximate counts as examination rules. [Official IELTS Listening format](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-academic-test/ielts-academic-format-listening).

### Feedback tools are learning aids

The video recommends AI and grammar tools for feedback. Treat their suggestions as material to review rather than a certified IELTS score. The official pages above describe assessment by qualified IELTS examiners. An automated rewrite also cannot show, by itself, whether you can produce that answer independently under test conditions.

### Advice that is personal rather than universal

His claims that Speaking is easy to improve, that interest-based reading prevents burnout, or that internal English thinking produces major gains describe his perspective. Preserve the useful practice ideas without treating every outcome as guaranteed.
